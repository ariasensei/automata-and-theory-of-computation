Big picture

Three questions of computer science

1. Can it be computed?

Computability: Is there an algorithm that always gives the required answer?

2. How efficiently?

Complexity: How much time or memory might it need?

3. Can we verify a solution quickly?

This question leads us toward P vs. NP.

Topic 1 • Turing Machines

The idea of a universal computing machine

A Turing machine is a mathematical model of a computer that reads and writes symbols, follows rules, and moves along a tape.

Real-world connection

Like a program following instructions one step at a time, a Turing machine changes its internal state as it processes data.

Topic 1 • The pioneer

Alan Turing (1912–1954)

1936

Introduced the abstract machine model to study what can be calculated by a mechanical procedure.

Why he matters

His work helped establish the foundations of modern computer science and computability.

Historical note: Turing also made major contributions to wartime codebreaking and early AI discussions.

Topic 1 • How it works

Five important parts

Tape

Stores symbols; theoretically unbounded.

Read/write head

Reads a symbol, writes a symbol, and moves.

States

Represent the machine's current situation.

Rules + stopping

Determine the next action; the machine may accept, reject, or run forever.

Topic 1 • Everyday analogy

A robot checking a list

Read itemApply ruleMark itemMove on

Example: Checking a form

A program scans fields one by one, checks required information, and reports whether the form is complete. This is an analogy—not a literal Turing machine implementation.

Topic 1 • Why it matters

What makes Turing machines powerful?

  • They give a precise meaning to the word algorithm.
  • They can model any computation normally considered algorithmically computable.
  • They help us prove the limits of computation.

Related pioneer: Alonzo Church independently developed a formal approach to computability using lambda calculus.

Topic 2 • Variants

Different designs, same basic power

Researchers studied modified Turing machines to make computations easier to describe and analyze.

Important principle

Standard deterministic, multi-tape, and nondeterministic Turing machines recognize the same class of computable languages, although their efficiency may differ.

Topic 2 • Multi-tape

Multi-tape Turing machine

Uses several tapes and heads, allowing the machine to organize information separately.

Real-world analogy: Multiple workspaces

Imagine comparing two documents while keeping a third page for notes. Separate workspaces simplify the procedure, even if one workspace could theoretically do the job.

Topic 2 • Nondeterministic

Nondeterministic Turing machine

At some steps, the model can have multiple possible next moves. An input is accepted if at least one possible computation accepts it.

Real-world analogy: Maze routes

Imagine exploring every possible route through a maze conceptually. If one route reaches the exit, the maze has a solution. This is not a claim that ordinary computers explore all routes instantly.

Topic 2 • Universal

Universal Turing machine

A single machine can simulate other Turing machines when given their descriptions and inputs.

Real-world connection: General-purpose computers

A laptop can run a browser, spreadsheet, game, or text editor by loading different programs instead of being rebuilt for every task.

Key contributor: Alan Turing (1936).

Topic 2 • Compare

Which variant is which?

Standard

One tape, one head, rule-based computation.

Multi-tape

Multiple workspaces; convenient organization.

Nondeterministic

Multiple possible computation paths.

Universal

Simulates a machine from its encoded description.

Topic 3 • Decidability

Can a program always answer?

A problem is decidable if an algorithm gives the correct yes/no answer and halts for every valid input.

Real-world example: Even or odd

Given an integer, a program can always determine whether it is even or odd and finish.

Topic 3 • People behind it

Church, Turing, and decision problems

Alonzo Church

Developed lambda calculus and showed limits on general decision procedures.

Alan Turing

Used machine computation to prove that some questions cannot be decided algorithmically.

Their 1936 results shaped modern computability theory.

Topic 3 • Examples

Examples of decidable problems

  • Is an integer divisible by 5?
  • Does a given string match a regular expression?
  • Does a finite graph contain a path between two specified vertices?

Where we see this

Input validation, text searching, and GPS route connectivity checks are examples of tasks with guaranteed decision procedures under well-defined inputs.

Topic 4 • Undecidability

Some questions have no universal solver

An undecidable problem has no algorithm that always halts with the correct yes/no answer for every possible valid input.

It does not mean every individual case is impossible to solve.

Topic 4 • The classic example

The Halting Problem

Can one program determine, for every possible program and input, whether that program will eventually stop?

Turing's result

No general-purpose algorithm can correctly decide this for all programs and inputs.

Topic 4 • Real world

Why can't a perfect bug detector exist?

Software tools can detect many bugs, infinite loops, and unsafe patterns. But a tool that always predicts termination correctly for every arbitrary program cannot exist.

Example

An app gets stuck in a loop for some inputs. A debugger may identify the cause in this case—but no single universal checker can resolve all such cases.

Topic 4 • Another milestone

Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem

David Hilbert and Wilhelm Ackermann helped formulate the challenge of finding a general decision method for first-order logic.

Church and Turing showed that no such general algorithm exists.

Big lesson

Not every precisely stated mathematical or computing question has a guaranteed algorithmic decision procedure.

Topic 5 • Complexity Theory

Solvable does not mean practical

Computational complexity studies how the resources required by an algorithm grow as the input becomes larger.

Time

How much computation is needed?

Space

How much memory is needed?

Topic 5 • Pioneers

Hartmanis, Stearns, and Cobham

  • Juris Hartmanis and Richard Stearns: foundational work on computational complexity (1965).
  • Alan Cobham: influential work connecting feasible computation with polynomial-time algorithms.
  • Stephen Cook and Leonid Levin: foundational contributions to NP-completeness.
Topic 5 • Easy comparison

Searching a list

Unsorted list

To find a name, you might check many entries one by one.

Sorted list

Binary search repeatedly halves the remaining search area.

Real-world example

Looking up a word in an alphabetized dictionary is faster than searching through a randomly arranged stack of words.

Topic 5 • Growth matters

Why large inputs change everything

An algorithm that works quickly on 10 items may become slow on a million items.

Example: Delivery routes

Finding the shortest route visiting many locations can become dramatically harder as more stops are added. Practical navigation tools often use smart approximations or specialized methods.

Key distinction: efficiency is about scaling, not just one stopwatch measurement.

Topic 6 • P vs. NP

Finding vs. checking a solution

P

Decision problems solvable in polynomial time by a deterministic algorithm.

NP

Decision problems whose proposed yes-solutions can be verified in polynomial time.

P is contained in NP. Whether P equals NP is still unknown.

Topic 6 • Everyday analogy

Sudoku: Solve it or check it?

For a completed Sudoku, checking whether the entries follow the rules is straightforward. Finding a valid completion may take much more work.

Important nuance

Ordinary 9×9 Sudoku is fixed-size. Complexity theory studies generalized Sudoku as puzzle sizes grow; generalized Sudoku is NP-complete.

Topic 6 • Pioneers

Stephen Cook and Leonid Levin

Stephen Cook (1971)

Established the NP-completeness of Boolean satisfiability (SAT).

Leonid Levin (1973)

Independently developed foundational results on universal search problems and NP-completeness.

Richard Karp (1972) showed many famous combinatorial problems are NP-complete.

Topic 6 • Real world

Where does P vs. NP matter?

Scheduling

Finding a conflict-free schedule with many constraints.

Logistics

Optimizing routes and assignments.

Chip design

Arranging components under many restrictions.

Security

Understanding hard computational problems; cryptography involves additional assumptions beyond P vs. NP.

Topic 6 • The open question

Is P = NP?

No one has proved whether P equals NP or not.

If P = NP, every problem with efficiently verifiable yes-solutions would also have an efficient algorithm for finding a yes/no answer.

The question is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems.

Key takeaways

Remember these distinctions

  • Computable: an algorithm can solve the problem.
  • Decidable: a yes/no algorithm always finishes.
  • Undecidable: no such universal decision algorithm exists.
  • Tractable: usually means efficiently solvable as inputs grow.
  • P vs. NP: a major unanswered question about efficient solving and verification.
